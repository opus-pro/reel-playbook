# Why people share: the reasoning behind the score

What each part of the entertainment score rests on, and how sure to be of it. Read once per session,
before the first scoring.

## The claim, and how much to trust it

The working claim: **a short travels when it is entertaining enough and makes people want to pass it
on, and the topic decides most of that.** Execution can waste a good topic; it rarely rescues a topic
nobody wants to send.

Weigh the evidence accordingly:

- **The mechanisms are well supported.** Curiosity as an information gap, high-arousal emotion
  driving sharing, and sharing as self-presentation come from decades of psychology and from studies
  of what people actually forward.
- **None of it was measured on Shorts or Reels.** The studies used news articles, lab tasks and
  surveys. Platform statements are what companies chose to say. Treat every link from these findings
  to a short-video score as a hypothesis.
- **The weights and the 70 bar are not calibrated yet.** They are a reasoned starting point. The
  user's own results (views ÷ subs, sends, follows) are what will correct them.

---

## 1. Aha: surprise that clicks

**What an aha feels like.** Research on insight describes the aha experience as sudden, easy once
seen, pleasant, and accompanied by a feeling that the answer is true. Each part maps to the rubric:
*sudden* is Surprise, *easy* and *true* are Click, and *pleasant* is why people want to repeat it to
someone else.

**Curiosity is a gap.** People feel curious when they notice a gap between what they know and what
they want to know. Two consequences:

- **The viewer needs a "before".** Curiosity is strongest when people half know the answer. A
  surprise about something familiar breaks an expectation; a surprise about something the viewer has
  never thought about has nothing to break. That is why the Surprise anchors favour familiar things.
- **A good answer opens a bigger gap.** Learning a little about a subject makes people more aware of
  what they don't know. That is the basis of Depth: the best payoffs leave a bigger question behind.

**The truth trap.** Studies of insight also found that an aha makes an idea *feel* true, including
when it is false. A confident, simple explanation of an unverified claim borrows that feeling. That
is why Click has a truth cap: the rubric rewards a deep topic said simply, never an unchecked claim
said confidently.

## 2. Share: what sending something says about the sender

**A like is cheap; a send has a cost.** A like is quiet approval. A send puts the sender's name on
the content in front of someone they know. People send things that make them look good, help someone,
or bring them closer to someone, and they hold back things that are merely pleasant, or embarrassing
to be seen forwarding. That is the like trap in one line: content can be likeable and still give the
sender nothing to say.

**Why people pass things on.** Reviews of word-of-mouth research group the reasons into five: to
manage how others see us, to share and process an emotion, to ask for information or opinions, to
bond with someone, and to persuade. Practical value (help) runs through several of them. The rubric's
six motives are those reasons, made concrete enough to write the message:

| Motive | Underlying reason | The message it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Look smart | Impression management: being informed, first to know | "Did you know this?" |
| This is so you | Social bonding | "@friend this is literally you" |
| You need this | Practical value, caring | "Watch this before you book your flight" |
| Can you believe this | Sharing a strong emotion | "I can't stop thinking about this" |
| Settle this | Persuasion, asking for opinions | "Who's right here?" |
| LOL | Bonding through a shared emotion | "😭😭" sent to the group chat |

**Arousal beats pleasantness.** A study of which news articles readers emailed most found that
content evoking high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) was shared more, and low-arousal sadness
less. Positive content was shared somewhat more than negative, and practically useful and surprising
content more than average. The lesson for shorts: strength of feeling matters more than its
direction, and "nice" is a low-arousal feeling.

**Triggers.** Things tied to something people see often get thought about, and passed on, again
later. A surprise about an everyday object has a built-in trigger: every time the viewer sees the
object, the video comes back to mind. That is the 9-10 anchor for Surprise.

**Public.** People copy what they can see others doing. A format that viewers can recognise and
repeat ("every week, an animal with a real job") spreads further than a one-off.

### What the platforms say

- **Instagram.** Its head, Adam Mosseri, said in January 2025 that watch time, likes per reach and
  sends per reach are the most important ranking signals, and that sends weigh more for reaching
  people who don't follow the account (as reported in coverage of his remarks). Some blogs go further
  and call sends the single strongest signal; that stronger claim has no primary source we could find.
  Creators see sends ("shares") per reel in Instagram Insights.
- **YouTube.** YouTube does not publish share counts or how much shares weigh for Shorts. Views ÷
  channel subscribers is the best public stand-in for reaching strangers; creators see subscribers
  gained per Short in YouTube Studio.
- **TikTok.** Share counts are public on every video, which makes TikTok the best public source for
  checking the Share score, even when the user doesn't post there.

Platforms change their ranking often. Check the date on any platform claim before leaning on it.

## 3. More: why some videos lead to the next one

- **Curiosity begets curiosity.** A payoff that reveals a bigger gap (section 1) sends people looking:
  the channel page, the comments, a search. A payoff that closes everything, as news usually does,
  ends the session.
- **A series is a promise.** A recognisable format tells the viewer what the next video will give
  them, which is a reason to follow. A one-off gives no reason.
- **Ownership.** If ten channels could post the same video, following any one of them is pointless.
  Access, expertise, a recurring character, a voice or a real take are what make a viewer want this
  channel's next one rather than anyone's.

The signals to check later: subscribers gained per 1,000 views (YouTube Studio), follows from the reel
(Instagram Insights), and how many viewers go on to the channel's other videos.

## 4. What the score cannot see

- **Execution.** A great topic can be told badly. That is the Hooks skill's half, and the reason the two
  scores are reported side by side.
- **Timing and luck.** The same concept posted a week later, or against a bigger story, can do very
  differently. The score judges the concept, not the day.
- **The audience's taste.** A channel's existing audience can love what strangers ignore (and the
  reverse). The channel's own outliers in `scout.py channel` are the correction.
