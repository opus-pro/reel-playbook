# Hooks and rehooks

The hook earns the first three seconds. Rehooks earn every seam after that: each place where one beat
ends and the viewer could feel done. This file holds the hook families with practical lines, tests for
a hook, before-and-after rewrites, and rehooks: what they are, where they go, and how to review them.
The hook package (line, frame 1, text, preview) and its scoring live in `hook-story-playbook.md`.

## 1. What a hook has to do

In about a second, a hook does four jobs:

1. **Stop the thumb.** Frame 1 shows something the eye did not expect: a result, an action already
   under way, a number, an odd pairing.
2. **Orient.** A stranger knows what this is about, with no context.
3. **Open a gap.** There is now a question the viewer wants answered.
4. **Promise a payoff the video delivers.** The gap closes on screen, near the end.

A hook that does 1-3 but not 4 is clickbait. It wins the swipe and loses the retention graph, and the
rubric treats the broken promise as a BLOCKER.

**Families are levers; packages are the delivery.** A family says why a line stops a thumb. Every
candidate still has to become a full package and pass the playbook's scoring: stakes, payable, works
muted. The strongest hooks pull two levers at once, such as a stakes line with a proof number, or a
result-first frame with a doubter's line. When writing ten packages, spread them across at least four
families. Ten wordings of one idea is one hook.

## 2. The hook bank

| # | Family | The lever | Shape | Backfires when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Result first | The end is seen first, so the viewer stays for "how" | Open on the finished thing or the reaction, then rewind | The result is ordinary, or the process adds nothing |
| 2 | Mid-action | Motion and an unfinished moment, with no warm-up | Frame 1 is already inside the action | The action is unreadable with the sound off |
| 3 | Stakes and constraint | A goal, a limit and a cost if it fails | "I have [limit] to [goal], or [consequence]" | The stakes are not believable |
| 4 | Doubter | Someone says it can't be done; we wait for the proof | "[Person] says [doubt]. So I [test]." | The doubter is a strawman nobody holds |
| 5 | Comparison with a hidden verdict | Two options, one winner, revealed at the end | "[cheap] vs [expensive]. [Judge] picks, blind." | The answer is obvious |
| 6 | Specific question | A gap the viewer can name | "Why does [familiar thing] [odd fact]?" | The answer is obvious, never comes, or comes in two seconds |
| 7 | Proof number | A specific figure is hard to scroll past | The number on screen first, the explanation after | The number cannot be checked, or three numbers compete |
| 8 | Contrarian | Tension with something the viewer believes | "Everyone does X. Here's what happens when you don't." | There is no better answer behind the take |
| 9 | Call-out | Feeling singled out | "If you [specific situation], this is for you." | It calls out everyone, or a group too small to matter |
| 10 | Relatable fail or POV | Recognition: "that's me" | A specific, slightly painful moment, then a turn | It is stock relatability that fits any account, with no turn |
| 11 | Fast payoff | A tiny time cost for a clear result | "[time]. [result]. Watch." | The video cannot deliver in that time |
| 12 | Transformation | The "before", with the "after" promised | Frame 1 is the before; the after flashes in at about 1.5 s | The after is shown too late to pull |
| 13 | Send-to-a-friend | Social currency: sending it makes the sender look good | Built around one recipient: "the friend who [habit]" | It asks for the share instead of earning it |
| 14 | Story opener (callback) | A specific person and a past moment imply a story about to pay off: "and then?" | "[Time ago], [specific person] [did a specific thing]." | Nothing specific anchors it ("so this happened"), or the stakes stay unnamed past about 5 s |

**Story openers need a known narrator or a fast turn.** Family 14 opens a gap without naming stakes:
the viewer trusts that a specific person plus a past moment leads somewhere. That trust is strongest
when the audience knows the narrator. Show the anchoring object on screen (the note, the photo),
and name the stakes within about 5 s ("…so I made it real"). A story opener whose stakes wait until
18 s is a slow hook, however good the story.

**Breadth versus aim.** Families 9 and 13 narrow the audience on purpose. That suits a niche with a
big audience, or a platform where sends drive reach. The playbook's Broad check still applies: if
outsiders would scroll past, pair the call-out with a stakes line (family 3), or zoom out.

## 3. Example lines by family

Every line here was written for this file. Adapt the shape; don't reuse the line.

**1. Result first**
- (Frame 1: a sneaker sliced in half, showing sponge cake inside.) "This is a cake. My brother tried
  to put it on."
- (Frame 1: a finished kitchen, "$28" on screen.) "This whole kitchen redo cost $28. Every receipt is
  in here."

**2. Mid-action**
- (Frame 1: an egg already falling toward a cushion of straws.) "Third try. If this one cracks, I owe
  him $20."
- (Frame 1: hands halfway through sawing a watermelon.) "Halfway through, I realised this was a
  mistake."

**3. Stakes and constraint**
- "I have one hour to sell 50 lemonades, or the trip is off."
- "One ingredient, three meals. My roommate rates every one."

**4. Doubter**
- "My dad says a $60 umbrella is a scam."
- "My coach says I can't hold a plank for five minutes. Today he's timing it."

**5. Comparison with a hidden verdict**
- "$3 vs $60 umbrella. Dad picks, blind."
- "Store brand vs name brand cereal. My kids can't see the box."

**6. Specific question**
- "Why do planes dim the cabin lights before landing?"
- "Why do onions make you cry, and how do chefs stop it?"

**7. Proof number**
- (Text "$0.41" appears first, then the voice.) "Forty-one cents a meal. Here's the whole week."
- (Text "4 → 1".) "Four apps down to one. Here's what I deleted."

**8. Contrarian**
- "Everyone says buy the big box. At this store, the big box costs more per ounce."
- "Stop meal-prepping on Sunday. Wednesday works better, and here's why."

**9. Call-out**
- "If you just moved out and own exactly one pan, this is for you."
- "If your plants die every winter, it's probably the window, not you."

**10. Relatable fail or POV**
- "I said 'I'll start Monday' for nine Mondays. Here's what finally worked."
- "POV: you budgeted perfectly, then your friend said 'let's just split it evenly.'"

**11. Fast payoff**
- "Fold a fitted sheet in 15 seconds. Watch."
- "Ten seconds, and you'll never peel garlic the slow way again."

**12. Transformation**
- (Frame 1: a scratched $20 thrift desk; at 1.5 s, the finished desk.) "Twenty dollars and one
  weekend."

**13. Send-to-a-friend**
- "This is for the friend who's always 'five minutes away'."
- "For the friend who reads every review before buying anything over $10."

**14. Story opener (callback)**
- (Frame 1: a crumpled napkin with a phone number on it.) "Two years ago, a stranger at the airport
  gave me this. Today I called it."
- (Frame 1: an old photo of a storefront, held up in front of the same storefront, now closed.) "Ten
  years ago, this shop gave me my first job. Yesterday it shut down."

## 4. One hook, four channels

| Channel | Its job | Practical rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Picture** (frame 1 and the first motion) | Stops the thumb before any sound plays | Follow the first-frame spec in the playbook. Show the result, the action or the number. Never a logo, a countdown or a warm-up shot. A face the audience already knows can be the subject; a stranger's face needs an object, number or text beside it. |
| **On-screen text** | Carries the hook for muted viewers | One line, three words or one number, inside the safe zones. It is a headline, not a caption: captions transcribe, the text hook sells. |
| **Voice** | States the stakes | One breath, about 2 s, conversational. Record three takes and keep the one that sounds least read. |
| **Sound** | Signals that something is happening | Start on frame 1 with the action's own sound (a crack, a sizzle, a gasp) rather than a music swell. |

All four point at the same question. When the text says one thing and the voice another, the muted
viewer and the listening viewer are watching two different videos, and neither gets the whole hook.

## 5. Tests for a hook

Run these on every finalist, and on the hook of any video under review.

| Test | How | It fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **First-second cut** | Delete the first second and watch again. Repeat until it breaks. | It still works without that second. Then that second was warm-up, so cut it. |
| **Mute** | Watch 0-3 s with the sound off. | A muted viewer can't say what the video is about and why to stay. |
| **One breath** | Say the line aloud at normal speed. | You need a second breath, or it runs past about 2.5 s. |
| **Stranger** | Picture someone who has never seen the channel. | It needs a name, an in-joke or earlier context. |
| **Payable** | Point to the timestamp where the promise is paid. | You can't, or the answer is weaker than the promise. |
| **Number first** | Check when a hook's number appears. | It is explained before it is shown. Show it, then explain it. |
| **Swap** | Put the hook on another video from the same channel. | It fits just as well, so it is generic. |
| **Gap length** | Find where the hook's question gets answered. | It is answered in the first 5 s with nothing opened after it (see rehooks), or never. |

In a review, the analyzer measures part of this: LATE_FIRST_WORD, HOOK_LINE_LONG, HOOK_STATIC,
FILLER_PHRASE. The first-second cut test is judged on the hook sheet: would the video lose anything
if its first 10 frames were gone?

## 6. Before and after

Each rewrite names the problem, then gives the whole package.

**Grocery budget**
- Before: "Hey guys, today I'm going to show you how I save money on groceries."
- Problem: a greeting and a signpost. No stakes, no number, nothing to see.
- After (proof number + result first): Line: "Our family of four ate for a week on $60." Frame 1: the
  week's groceries on a table, the receipt in front, "$60" big. Preview at 1.3 s: the last dinner
  being served.

**Phone review**
- Before: "This is my full review of the new phone after two weeks."
- Problem: a topic only fans care about, with no question in it.
- After (doubter + comparison): Line: "My camera-snob friend says no phone beats her old camera. We
  shot the same photo on both, blind." Frame 1: the phone and the camera side by side with a "?"
  between them. Preview: her face at the reveal.

**Fitness**
- Before: "Here are five tips for better push-ups."
- Problem: a list with no stakes. Tip 1 has to be strong enough to buy tips 2-5, and nothing says it
  is.
- After (stakes + proof number): Line: "I couldn't do one push-up. Thirty days later: 25." Frame 1:
  a split screen, day 1 face-down on the mat and day 30 mid-rep, with "0 → 25". Preview: rep 25.

**Travel**
- Before: "Let's explore Tokyo's best hidden spots!"
- Problem: "let's explore" is a signpost, and "best hidden spots" is a promise every travel video
  makes.
- After (constraint + specific promise): Line: "One ¥1,000 note for a whole day in Tokyo. The last
  stop was free." Frame 1: a ¥1,000 note held up at a busy crossing. Preview: the last stop.

**Software (B2B)**
- Before: "Introducing our new invoicing feature."
- Problem: a product announcement is a topic. Nobody outside the user base cares.
- After (relatable fail + result first): Line: "I used to spend Friday nights chasing unpaid
  invoices. This is my Friday now." Frame 1: a laptop with a red "OVERDUE ×14" list that turns green
  at 1.5 s. Preview: the green list.

## 7. Rehooks

**What a rehook is.** A rehook is a small hook placed at a seam: the moment one beat resolves and the
viewer could feel finished. It closes that beat's question and opens the next one before the viewer
notices the first one closed.

> **Close, then open.** "It held. But that was only rain." The first sentence pays off the beat. The
> second opens the next one.

**Why it works.** Every answered question is an exit. Viewers leave when they feel done (their
question was answered) or when nothing new is coming. Drops in a retention graph tend to come right
after an answer or a section break, so the seams are where rehooks earn their place.

**Hook versus rehook.** The hook opens the video's big question, which is only answered at the
payoff. Rehooks open small questions that are answered within a beat or two, so the viewer always has
one open question on the way to the big one.

### Timing

- **Long-form** (Marquise Peters' rule of thumb): about every 30-40 s. Use them more often in the
  first third (every 20-30 s) and less often near the end, where the remaining viewers are committed.
  Never go more than about 45 s without one. These are one practitioner's numbers; test them.
- **Shorts.** A whole 33 s short is shorter than one long-form interval, so place rehooks by beat,
  not by the clock: one at every seam. In practice that is the end of the setup (about 3-6 s), then
  every 5-8 s through the mechanism. The front-loading still holds: the first seam after the hook is
  the most dangerous, so the first rehook comes early. The last 2-3 s need none, because the payoff
  answers the big question.
- **The limit Hooks uses.** In a short, no more than about 10 s without a new question. The
  limit grows with length up to the ~45 s ceiling for long videos: `analyze.py` uses the video's
  length divided by 6, kept between 10 and 45 s. It is a heuristic, so calibrate it against the
  user's own retention graphs.

### Said, shown or both

A rehook can be a line ("then the stove died"), a picture (the counter drops to $6, a new obstacle
walks in), or both. Shown rehooks reach muted viewers. Said rehooks reach viewers who are looking
away. The strongest do both, with the cut landing on the turn word.

## 8. Rehook types, with lines

| Type | What it opens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Escalation | Something bigger is coming | "And that was the easy one." |
| New obstacle | The stakes go up | "Then the stove died." |
| Twist | The expected outcome flips | "It worked. That was the problem." |
| Counter update | Progress against the limit, shown on screen | "$6 left. Two meals to go." |
| Warning | A cost for missing what comes next | "Skip this step and the whole thing slides off." |
| Mistake reveal | The trap the viewer would fall into | "This is where I wasted my first $20." |
| Specific promise | A named thing still to come | "The last one is the only one Dad liked." |
| Prediction | The viewer's own guess, to be checked | "Guess which one she picks." |
| Pattern break | Amplifies a turn: a punch-in, a new place, a sound hit, a text pop | (picture and sound) |

A pattern break alone is not a rehook. A cut or a whoosh with no new question is decoration (see
"transitions as decoration" in `transitions.md`). Put the break on the turn word of a real rehook.

## 9. Rehook rules and faults

**Rules**

- **At the seam, not mid-beat.** Place a rehook where a beat resolves. A rehook in the middle of a
  beat interrupts the answer the viewer is waiting for.
- **Pay off every one.** A rehook is a small promise, and the next beat must keep it. "But wait"
  followed by nothing new is clickbait in miniature, and it teaches viewers to ignore the next one.
- **Specific beats generic.** A generic tease works once. "The last one is the one that broke" names
  what is coming, so it pulls every time.
- **Vary the form.** "But" five times becomes a tic. Mix the types, and mix said with shown.
- **Keep it short.** In a short, a rehook is one clause: about 3-7 words, under a
  second.
- **Make it work muted.** Put the turn in on-screen text or in the picture (the counter, the
  obstacle), not only in the voice.
- **Cut on the turn word.** Land a visual change on "but", "then" or "until", so the turn is seen as
  well as heard.
- **One per seam.** A cliffhanger on every line slows the story and reads as manipulation.
- **Nothing after the payoff.** Once the big question is answered, end. "But that's not all" after the
  payoff kills the loop. The exception is a sentence-loop ending, which opens a question that the
  first line answers on replay.

**Faults to name in a review**

| Fault | What it looks like | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Missing | A seam with no new question; the retention graph slides there | **ADD**: write the line or plan the shot |
| Fake | Opens a question the video never answers | **PAY OFF** later, or **CUT** |
| Generic | A tease that would fit any video | **SHARPEN**: name what is coming |
| Signpost | Announces instead of opening ("next, step two", "now let's talk about") | **SHARPEN**: "step two is where it broke" |
| Buried | The turn sits mid-sentence or under a long line | **MOVE** it to the start of a short line, and cut on it |
| Sound-only | Only the voice carries it | **SHOW** it as text or in the picture |
| Late | The beat resolved, then the picture idled before the turn | **TRIM** the idle, or **MOVE** the turn earlier |
| After the payoff | Opens something new once the big answer is out | **CUT**, and end on the answer |

## 10. The rehook map

Build one in Create mode (as a column of the beat sheet) and in Review mode (after the story spine).

| # | Time | Beat that ends | Closes | Opens | Said / shown | Gap since last | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

In a review:

1. Start from the "Rehook candidates" section of `report.md`. The analyzer lists the spoken turns
   (such as but, until, turns out) and questions, with their times. It also gives the longest
   stretch without one, and REHOOK_GAP flags any stretch longer than the limit.
2. Confirm each candidate against the transcript. Many uses of "but" open nothing.
3. Add the shown rehooks from the timeline sheets: counters, new obstacles, reveals. Words can't
   catch these.
4. Give each seam a verdict: KEEP, ADD, SHARPEN, SHOW, MOVE, TRIM, PAY OFF or CUT.
5. Any stretch longer than the limit with nothing said or shown is a finding. Name the seam where the
   rehook belongs, and write the line.

For a script with no video, `readability.py` lists the same candidates with estimated times.

## 11. Worked example: a 33 s short with every hook and rehook marked

This is the umbrella short from the playbook.

| Time | Beat | Picture | Voice | Text | Hook or rehook |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | Frame 1 | Dad holding two closed umbrellas crossed like swords, price tags showing | | "$3 vs $60" | **Hook**: comparison + doubter |
| 0.0-2.0 | Hook line | Dad | "My dad says a $60 umbrella is a scam." | | Hook line |
| 1.2-1.8 | Preview | The $3 umbrella flips inside out | (line continues over it) | | Payoff preview |
| 2.0-5.0 | Setup | Both umbrellas in the yard, a bin behind them | "So we test both. Loser goes in the trash." | "3 rounds" | **Rehook 1** at 4.5 (stakes): which one ends up in the bin? |
| 5.0-11.0 | Round 1: rain | A garden hose | "Rain. Both fine. Rain is easy." | "1-1" | **Rehook 2** at 10.5 (escalation): what comes after easy? |
| 11.0-18.0 | Round 2: wind | A fan; the cheap umbrella bends | "The cheap one bends... and holds?" | "2-2" | **Rehook 3** at 17.0 (twist): did the cheap one just win? |
| 18.0-27.0 | Round 3 | Dad wheels in a leaf blower | "Then Dad got the leaf blower." | "FINAL" | **Rehook 4** at 18.5 (new obstacle, shown and said), cut on "Then" |
| 27.0-31.5 | Payoff | One umbrella standing; Dad's face | "...Okay." | | Answers the hook |
| 31.5-33.5 | Abrupt end | Cut on his face | | | No rehook after the payoff |

The seams fall at 4.5, 10.5, 17.0 and 18.5 s. The longest stretch without a new question is 18.5-27 s
(8.5 s), inside the 10 s limit, and the leaf blower round carries it as continuous action. Each
rehook is answered by the next beat, the types vary, and the score text makes every one visible to
muted viewers.

## 12. Testing hooks after posting

- **Change one thing.** Keep the body and change the first 3 s: a new frame 1, or a new line. Change
  two things and you learn nothing.
- **Read the right number.** The hook moves the early metric: YouTube's viewed vs swiped away, or the
  share of viewers who stay past 3 s on Reels. Rehooks change the shape of the middle of the retention
  graph.
- **Instagram Trial Reels** go to non-followers first, so a risky hook can be tested on strangers
  without the goodwill of followers. The Socialmon guide suggests reading results after about 24
  hours, and says a trial that does well within 72 hours can be shared to followers automatically.
  Check how it currently behaves in the app, because it changes.
- **One test is one data point.** Repeat a pattern over a few videos before calling it a rule.
- **Log every test**: the family, frame 1, the line and the early metric. The next brief can then
  start from the channel's own data.
